🤖 What makes a robot a robot?

A beginner-friendly introduction to the main components found in many robots, how they work together, and how to identify them in real systems.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this tutorial, you should be able to:
  • Define a robot in simple terms.
  • Name and explain the main components of a robot.
  • Distinguish between sensors, a controller, actuators, power supply and the robot's structure.
  • Describe how information and movement flow through a robot.
  • Identify components in a simple mobile robot.

What is a robot?

A robot is a machine that can sense information from its environment, process that information, and perform actions. Some robots work automatically, while others receive instructions from a human operator.

Think of a robot like a human body: sensors are like eyes and ears, the controller is like the brain, actuators are like muscles, and the frame is like the skeleton.

Illustrative robot — actual designs vary according to their purpose.

Warm-up: Think and discuss

Is every machine a robot? Discuss with a partner. Compare a simple electric fan, an automatic vacuum cleaner and a remote-controlled toy car.

Not every machine is a robot. A basic fan performs a fixed function. A robot generally has some combination of sensing, control and action. A remote-controlled car is a robot-like machine, but the level of autonomy depends on how much it can do without a human controlling it.
Presenter guidance: Start with familiar examples. Ask learners what a robot needs in order to move, avoid an obstacle or pick up an object. Accept ideas first, then connect them to the component names.

🧩 The main components of a robot

A robot is a system made up of parts that work together. The exact parts depend on the robot's task and design.

Five important component groups

1. Sensors — gather information

Sensors detect changes or objects in the robot's surroundings, or measure the robot's own condition.

Examples: ultrasonic distance sensor, camera, infrared sensor, touch sensor, temperature sensor, encoder and gyroscope.

Purpose: Help the robot know what is happening.

2. Controller — makes decisions

The controller receives sensor data, follows a program and sends commands to other components.

Examples: Arduino, Raspberry Pi, microcontroller, PLC or an industrial robot controller.

Purpose: Acts as the robot's control centre or brain.

3. Actuators — create movement or action

Actuators convert energy and control signals into physical movement or another action.

Examples: DC motors, servo motors, stepper motors, pneumatic cylinders and hydraulic actuators.

Purpose: Move wheels, arms, grippers or other mechanisms.

4. Power supply — provides energy

The power supply provides the electrical or other energy needed by the controller, sensors and actuators.

Examples: rechargeable battery, DC power supply or mains supply.

Purpose: Keeps the robot's systems operating.

5. Mechanical structure — supports the robot

The structure holds components in place and provides the physical shape of the robot.

Examples: frame, chassis, wheels, links, joints, brackets and gripper.

Purpose: Supports components and transfers forces and movement.

Also important: communication and software

Software contains the instructions that control the robot. Communication links allow components or robots to exchange information.

Examples: program code, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, serial communication, CAN bus and Ethernet.

Purpose: Coordinate operations and exchange data.

Remember: Sensors detect. The controller processes. Actuators act. The power supply provides energy. The structure holds everything together.

🔄 How the components work together

A robot usually follows a cycle: sense the environment, process information, then perform an action.

The basic robot control cycle

1. SENSORS
Collect information
→
2. CONTROLLER
Process information
↓
3. ACTUATORS
Perform an action
←

The robot may sense again after acting. This repeated cycle allows it to respond to changes in its environment.

Everyday example: When you walk towards a wall, your eyes detect the wall, your brain processes the information, and your muscles help you stop or change direction. A robot can use a sensor, controller and motors in a similar way.

Example: a robot avoiding an obstacle

  1. An ultrasonic sensor measures the distance to an object.
  2. The controller reads the distance measurement.
  3. The program checks whether the object is too close.
  4. The controller sends a command to the motor driver.
  5. The motors slow down, stop or turn the robot.
  6. The sensor measures again to check the new surroundings.
Important: A motor driver is an interface between a controller and a motor. It allows the controller's low-power signal to control the higher current required by the motor. It is not the same thing as the motor itself.

🛠️ Identify components in real robots

Different robots use different combinations of components. The design is chosen to suit the task.

Mobile robot / rover

  • Ultrasonic sensor or LiDAR: detects obstacles.
  • Controller: processes sensor readings and navigation instructions.
  • Motor driver: controls the motors.
  • DC gear motors: turn the wheels.
  • Battery: supplies electrical power.
  • Chassis and wheels: support and move the robot.

Industrial robotic arm

  • Controller: coordinates the arm's movements.
  • Motors and gearboxes: move the joints.
  • Encoders: provide position feedback.
  • Gripper or tool: performs the task.
  • Frame and links: form the arm's structure.
  • Power supply: supplies the required energy.

Line-following robot

  • Infrared sensors: detect the line.
  • Microcontroller: decides which direction to move.
  • Motor driver: controls motor power.
  • DC motors: drive the wheels.
  • Battery and chassis: power and support the robot.

Drone

  • Flight controller: stabilises and controls flight.
  • Gyroscope and accelerometer: measure rotation and acceleration.
  • Motors and propellers: generate thrust.
  • Battery: supplies energy.
  • Frame: holds the components together.

Scenario: College delivery rover

A college wants a small rover to carry books from the library to a classroom. It must detect obstacles, move along a route and stop at the destination.

Discuss: Which sensor could help it detect an obstacle? Which component makes decisions? Which components make it move? What provides power?

An ultrasonic sensor or LiDAR can detect obstacles. A microcontroller or single-board computer can process the information. A motor driver and motors move the rover. A battery supplies power, while the chassis supports the payload and components. A camera or other navigation sensor may also help it follow a route.
Presenter guidance: Invite learners to compare the rover and robotic arm. Ask: “Why does a robotic arm need joints and a gripper, while a rover needs wheels?” Link each component to the task the robot must perform.

Question: A robot detects a box using a camera, processes the image using a Raspberry Pi, and uses a servo motor to move a small arm. Identify the sensor, controller and actuator. Also name one other component needed to make the robot operate.

The camera is the sensor, the Raspberry Pi is the controller, and the servo motor is the actuator. The robot also needs a power supply. It would need a mechanical structure such as an arm and frame to support the parts.

Lesson summary

  • Sensors collect information.
  • Controllers process information and issue commands.
  • Actuators create movement or action.
  • Power supplies provide energy.
  • Mechanical structures support the robot and its mechanisms.
  • Software and communication coordinate the robot's operation.

Exit ticket: In one sentence, explain how a robot uses a sensor, controller and actuator to respond to its environment.

Presenter guidance: Use the matching activity as a formative check. Ask learners to justify their answers rather than only naming a component. For the exit ticket, look for the sequence: sensor input → controller processing → actuator action.
